01 / The encoder

One model, one vector per review

A multilingual sentence encoder, chosen because it places Spanish paraphrases close together. Every review becomes a fixed-length list of numbers that later stages can measure distances on.

02 / The transformation

What the notebook does

Preparation keeps the text exactly as published; the encoder turns the list of strings into a matrix with one row per review. Condensed from the notebook's prepare_embedding_input, load_encoder and embed_sample cells.

# Prepare: plain text, one string per record; row order is the link to the rows
texts = prepared_sample["embedding_text"].tolist()

# Encode: 1,000 strings in, a 1,000 × 384 matrix out
encoder = SentenceTransformer(
    "sentence-transformers/paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2",
    revision="e8f8c211…",          # pinned, so the vectors are reproducible
    device="cpu",
)
embeddings = encoder.encode(
    texts,
    batch_size=64,
    normalize_embeddings=True,     # every row has length 1
    convert_to_numpy=True,
)
embeddings.shape                   # (1000, 384)

      How the similarity is computed

      Cosine similarity

      Two vectors are similar when they point in the same direction, whatever their length. The cosine of the angle between them measures exactly that:

      cos θ = (a · b) / (‖a‖ · ‖b‖)

      The encoder normalised every vector to length 1, so the denominator is 1 and the similarity is simply the dot product: 384 multiplications, added up.

      similarity = Σi = 1…384 ai · bi

      Waiting for the notebook's reduction step…

      01 / The problem

      We can measure closeness, but we cannot see it

      We now have a way of knowing how close two reviews are: the cosine similarity of their vectors. The next idea follows naturally. If we can find groups of vectors that sit close together, those groups should hold reviews about similar subjects, and those subjects are what we want to explore.

      The obstacle is that every vector has 384 dimensions, and we can only picture two or three at a time. Pick any two dimensions and a pair of reviews may look close; pick two others and the same pair looks far apart. No single pair of axes tells the truth, because the distance lives in all 384 coordinates at once. Clustering algorithms meet the same problem in another form: with so many dimensions, distances between points become more alike and groups harder to separate.

      This is where dimensionality reduction helps: it builds a few new axes that keep as much of the neighbourhood structure as possible, so that close means close wherever you look.

        Algorithm

        UMAP · keep each point's neighbours as its neighbours

        Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (McInnes, Healy & Melville, 2018) works in two steps. Build a graph: every point is linked to its n_neighbors nearest points in the original space, with a weight that fades with distance, so the graph records who is near whom. Lay the graph out: points get positions in the small space and are moved, step by step, so that linked points are pulled together and unlinked points are pushed apart, until the neighbourhoods in the new space match the graph as well as possible. n_neighbors decides whether the picture respects local detail (small) or global shape (large); min_dist sets how tightly neighbours may pack.

        Below, 42 mock points in two groups and one curved strand are reduced from two dimensions to one. The right strip is the layout; a well-separated band per group means the structure survived. This is a simplified re-enactment of the idea, not the production algorithm.

        Algorithm

        PCA · keep the directions where the data varies most

        Principal Component Analysis finds new axes, one at a time: the first is the straight line through the data's centre along which the points spread out the most; the second is perpendicular to it and captures the most of what remains, and so on. Keeping the first k axes and dropping the rest is the reduction. It is linear (every new coordinate is a weighted sum of the old ones), fast, and tells you exactly how much variance you kept. Its limit is also its definition: a curved or clustered structure that no straight axis captures is flattened, which is why the book prefers UMAP for clustering text embeddings.

        Below, 80 mock points in two dimensions are reduced to one by projecting them onto a line through their centre. Turn the line and watch how much of the spread survives; "Snap to PC1" jumps to the direction PCA would choose.

        Clustering: groups in the small space

        Waiting for the notebook's clustering step…

        Waiting for the notebook's clustering step…

        01 / The idea

        Dense regions are candidate subjects

        Each review is now a point in five dimensions whose distances mean something, so we can look for groups: regions where points are packed more tightly than their surroundings. A tight group of vectors is a set of reviews that say similar things, so every group is a candidate subject. A group gets a number, not a name; naming it is the next stage.

        The common choice, k-means, needs the number of groups in advance, and we do not know it. We use HDBSCAN instead, as the book does: a density-based method that discovers how many groups the data supports and leaves points that sit in no dense region unassigned rather than forcing them into a group. Its knobs are the smallest group it may report and how strict its density estimate is.

        Two cautions for reading what follows: group numbers are identifiers, not ranks, and a group is a candidate topic, not a topic yet.

        The sample, grouped

            k-means was told to find as many groups as HDBSCAN discovered. It assigns every review and draws straight boundaries through the dense mass of short praise; HDBSCAN draws around dense regions and leaves the odd reviews out. The silhouettes are not directly comparable: HDBSCAN's is computed only on the points it chose to assign.

            Algorithm

            HDBSCAN · groups are regions that stay dense

            A point is dense when it has at least min_samples neighbours within some reach. Dense points that can reach each other form a group; non-dense points within reach of a group are its border; everything else is noise. That is one reach. HDBSCAN looks at every reach at once: as the reach grows, groups appear, grow and merge, forming a hierarchy. It then keeps the groups that persist over the widest range of reaches (the "excess of mass" rule), discards anything smaller than min_cluster_size, and reports points that never belong to a persistent group as unassigned. Each member also gets a probability for how firmly it belongs.

            Below, 70 mock points: three blobs of different density and some scattered noise. Move the reach to see the single-threshold picture, or sweep it to watch groups appear and merge; the strip records how many groups exist at each reach, which is what HDBSCAN reads the persistent ones from.

            Algorithm

            k-means · k centres, everything assigned

            k-means starts with k centres, assigns every point to its nearest centre, moves each centre to the mean of its points, and repeats until nothing changes. It is fast and the groups are tidy, but it must be told k, its boundaries are straight lines between centres whatever shape the data has, and it assigns every point, including the odd ones. That is why the book prefers a density method for reviews, where the number of subjects is unknown and much of the text is noise.

            Same 70 mock points. Choose k, then step or run the iterations; diamonds are the centres. Compare the noise: here it always ends up inside a group.

            Topic modelling: the words that set a group apart

            Waiting for the notebook's topic step…

            Waiting for the notebook's topic step…

            01 / From groups to keywords

            Keywords are evidence, not names

            A group is a set of reviews that say similar things, but reading forty reviews to learn what they share does not scale. Topic modelling describes each group with a handful of keywords: the words that characterise it against the rest of the collection. Keywords are not a label; they are the evidence from which a person, or later a language model, names the topic.

            BERTopic is a modular framework with two halves. The first is exactly what we did: embed, reduce, cluster. The second turns every cluster into a topic representation, and each half is a replaceable block. We feed it our saved vectors and groups, so its topics are our groups, renumbered by size: topic 0 is the largest group, topic −1 the unassigned reviews.

            The basic representation block is a bag of words per group weighted by a class-based TF-IDF. Two vocabulary choices shape it: a short Spanish stop-word list ( words; negations such as no are kept, because no abre is a different complaint from abre) and bigrams, so a two-word phrase can be a keyword in its own right.

                    Block

                    The vectorizer · what counts as a word

                    Before anything is weighed, every review is lowercased and split into tokens of two or more letters or digits (scikit-learn's default rule: accents are kept, punctuation and single characters are dropped). Tokens on the stop-word list are removed, then bigrams are formed from what remains. Each cluster's bag of words is the count of these unigrams and bigrams over all its reviews.

                    Tokens · struck through = stop word

                    Bigrams · formed after stop words are removed

                    Block

                    c-TF-IDF · the class is the document

                    Classic TF-IDF asks, for a word, in how many reviews does it appear? and penalises it with log(N / df). c-TF-IDF joins each group into one document and asks instead how much does it appear across the groups?, weighting by log(1 + A / ft), where A is the average words per group and ft the word's total count. The difference matters for reviews: a one-word review repeated forty times inside a group is forty documents for the classic formula, but one dense group for the class-based one.

                    01 / From keywords to a label

                    Ask once per topic, never per review

                    Keywords are evidence; a label is a claim. The last Lego block asks a language model to make that claim, and the trick that keeps it cheap and auditable is scope: the model sees one topic at a time, only its keywords and a handful of representative reviews, and it is called once per topic. Thirty-seven calls describe a thousand reviews; the same recipe describes a million.

                    Our prompt is the repository's Spanish prompt for app reviews. It declares the reviews untrusted data, never instructions; it forces a JSON answer with a proposed feature, a description, the ids of the reviews that support it and an assessment: single_feature when the evidence points at one user-visible capability, mixed when it spans several, unclear when it is not enough. A proposed feature is only allowed in the first case, so an empty label is restraint, not failure. Every citation must be one of the reviews sent, or the answer is rejected.

                    Before the call, two cheaper blocks improved the keywords the model sees: a KeyBERT-inspired reranking by meaning and maximal marginal relevance for variety. Both are optional and independent; open them below. Answers are cached by model, prompt version and evidence, so reruns never call the API.

                    Each shows our real keyword lists before and after the block, for any topic.

                      Thirty-seven answers, read with their assessment

                      A feature headline appears only where the model judged the evidence to point at one capability; everywhere else the description is the summary. Click a row to open its exchange above.

                        Reading the pattern: the large praise groups (excelente, muy buena) come back unclear because they carry sentiment, not a feature. That is a finding, not a defect: topics and sentiment are different axes, which is why the next stage measures sentiment on its own.

                        Block

                        KeyBERT-inspired · rerank by meaning

                        c-TF-IDF ranks words by counts. The KeyBERT-inspired block takes the top candidates of each topic, embeds them with the same sentence encoder we used for the reviews, embeds the topic's representative reviews, and reorders the candidates by cosine similarity to the topic. Nothing new is added; words that mean what the group is about move up, and typos or fragments that merely occur often move down. It costs one small embedding batch per topic.

                        Block

                        Maximal marginal relevance · rerank for variety

                        A list of ten keywords that are all variants of one word tells you one thing ten times. MMR builds the list one word at a time: each pick maximises similarity to the topic minus diversity × similarity to the words already picked. At diversity 0 it is plain relevance; at 1 it only cares about being different. We chain it after the KeyBERT block with thirty candidates, so it has something to choose from.

                        01 / What we measure

                        One label per review, then counted per topic

                        Topics say what people talk about; sentiment says how they feel while saying it. Each review is classified as a whole into negative, neutral or positive by RoBERTuito, a Spanish RoBERTa fine-tuned for sentiment by the pysentimiento project, pinned to one revision and run on CPU. The review goes through the model authors' Spanish preprocessing, is truncated at 128 tokens, and comes back as three probabilities; the label is the largest. Only the text enters the classifier, never the topic's keywords or its generated label.

                        A topic's sentiment is the mix of its reviews, each counted once. It is not a judgement about the label: a single-feature topic can be mostly negative (a complaint) or mostly positive (praise). The probabilities are not calibrated, so we report counts and shares rather than averages of confidence. And this is whole-review sentiment, not aspect-based: a review that praises transfers and curses the login gets one label.

                              Model

                              RoBERTuito · three numbers per review

                              The classifier is a RoBERTa-style transformer pre-trained on Spanish tweets and fine-tuned to predict sentiment. A review is tokenised, read by the network, and reduced to three scores, one per class. A softmax turns the scores into probabilities that sum to one, and the largest wins. The probabilities say which class the model prefers and by how much, not how often it is right; that is what "uncalibrated" means.
